Predicting Personal Loan Acceptance 🏦

accuracy = 0.97 · recall = 0.96 · sklearn DecisionTree, post-pruned expand +collapse −

Converting existing bank depositors into loan customers is a significant revenue opportunity, yet marketing campaigns can be costly and inefficient. This project presents a data science approach to pinpoint which customers have the highest probability of accepting a personal loan, enabling smarter, more targeted marketing efforts.

The challenge: from data to decision

The analysis centers on a dataset from Thera Bank, which, like many financial institutions, wants to grow its loan business by marketing to its large base of deposit customers. With a previous campaign yielding a 9% conversion rate, the goal is to build a model that can significantly improve this result by identifying the right customers to target.

The project leverages a rich dataset of 5,000 customers, with attributes including demographics (Age, Family size), financial standing (Income, CCAvg, Mortgage), and their relationship with the bank (CD Account, Securities Account).

Key steps and insights

Data cleaning: removing irrelevant columns like ID, converting categorical data into the correct format, and handling inconsistencies. A notable finding was that 52 younger customers had negative values for Experience, determined to be a data entry error and corrected by taking the absolute value.

Exploratory analysis: income showed a strong positive correlation with loan acceptance; customers with higher education levels were significantly more likely to accept; and holding a Certificate of Deposit account was also a strong indicator.

Modelling: Logistic Regression and a Decision Tree Classifier were trained, with the Decision Tree proving particularly effective. It was optimised using post-pruning to prevent overfitting. The final model achieved 97% accuracy and 96% recall — high recall matters here because it means the model catches 96% of all customers who would actually accept a loan, minimising missed opportunities for the bank.

Conclusion

The most influential factors were Income, Education Level, and Family Size. By focusing marketing on customers who fit this profile, the bank can significantly increase campaign conversion rates while reducing marketing spend — and the same methodology adapts readily to mortgages, credit cards, or investment products.

Building a Movie Recommender 🎬

TF-IDF + cosine similarity · SVD collaborative filter · RMSE ≈ 0.89 expand +collapse −

Ever wonder how Netflix knows you'd love that obscure documentary? The magic is in recommender systems, a type of information filtering that has become the backbone of major tech companies. This project dives into the world of movie recommendations, building a series of engines that grow in complexity and performance, using the TMDB 5000 Movie Dataset.

1. Demographic filtering: the "top charts" approach

The simplest approach offers generalised recommendations based on popularity, using the IMDB weighted rating formula to balance a movie's average rating with its vote count. It produces a reliable Top 10 of critically acclaimed films — great movies, but not personalised in any way.

2. Content-based filtering: "if you liked this, you'll like that"

Content-based systems use a movie's metadata to find similar titles. Two versions were built: a plot-based recommender using TF-IDF vectors and cosine similarity, and a richer version combining the top 3 actors, director, genres, and keywords. Recommending from The Dark Knight Rises, the metadata-rich version correctly surfaced other Christopher Nolan films including Inception.

3. Collaborative filtering: the power of the crowd

The most advanced technique matches people with similar tastes rather than relying on metadata. Using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) via the surprise library, the model predicts a user's rating for unseen movies, achieving an RMSE of approximately 0.89.

Conclusion

The project illustrates the trade-offs between simplicity and personalisation. The key takeaway: the most powerful systems are hybrid, combining the metadata-awareness of content-based filtering with the crowd-sourced intelligence of collaborative filtering.

Real-Time Car Counter with YOLO & SORT 🚗

YOLOv8m detection · SORT + Kalman filter tracking · OpenCV expand +collapse −

Manually counting objects is tedious and prone to error. This computer vision project offers a robust, automated solution for counting cars in a video feed, showcasing how modern machine learning models can be combined into a practical, real-world application.

1. Object detection with YOLOv8

The foundation is YOLO (You Only Look Once), a state-of-the-art real-time object detection model. The script loads the YOLOv8m weights, balancing speed and accuracy, and draws bounding boxes around every vehicle it recognises in each frame.

2. Object tracking with SORT

Detecting cars per-frame isn't enough — you'd count the same car repeatedly. The SORT algorithm assigns each vehicle a unique ID and uses a Kalman filter to predict its position in the next frame, maintaining identity across frames. This persistent tracking is the key to accurate counting.

3. The counting mechanism

A virtual line is drawn across the road; when a tracked vehicle's centre point crosses it, the counter increments and the ID is stored so it's never counted twice. The running total displays on screen in real time.

Broader applications

  • Retail analytics: footfall counting and queue monitoring
  • Industrial automation: counting products on a conveyor for quality control
  • Security: monitoring entries and exits in restricted areas
  • Wildlife conservation: population studies without human interference
  • Smart cities: pedestrian flow analysis and parking management
