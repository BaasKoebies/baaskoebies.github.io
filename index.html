Should we run this retention change? No: it loses R11.3M even if it works perfectly

telecoms · retention economics · upper-bound argument · R11.3M net loss averted expand +collapse −

Decision

A telecoms client proposed a product-configuration change to reduce churn across a base in the 100k to 1M range. Commercial leadership needed to decide whether to roll it out.

Answer

Do not run it. Over the client's standard three-month window the change gives up R35.2M in revenue. The most it could protect, if every single churner stayed, is R23.9M. Net: minus R11.3M. The proposal was shelved.

Baseline

The case for the change rested entirely on churn reduction. The usual approach is to estimate how much churn the change would prevent, which is the number everyone then argues about. I skipped the argument: I assumed the change prevents all churn and asked whether it pays for itself even then.

Method

  • A, revenue given up: the revenue the configuration change forgoes over three months. R35.2M.
  • B, maximum revenue protected: monthly churners multiplied by three-month average revenue per user. This assumes 100% retention, so it is a ceiling, not an estimate. R23.9M.
  • Same three-month horizon on both sides. Revenue on both sides. All inputs are three-month averages.

Net = B − A = R23.9M − R35.2M = −R11.3M

Result

No churn outcome closes the gap. To break even, B would have to be 47% above its own ceiling. The decision did not depend on any churn forecast, which is why it held up.

Sensitivity

  • Margin instead of revenue? Margin shrinks both terms and leaves the sign unchanged.
  • Other upside? Churn reduction was the only benefit claimed, and it was granted in full.
  • What would flip it? New revenue, not retained revenue: R11.3M of net new revenue over three months, or the equivalent in net new subscribers at the three-month ARPU.

Limitations

Anonymised, so the components are shown but not their derivation from raw data. The analysis prices the proposal as written; it does not estimate what a cheaper or targeted version would do. That question became the next project.

What happened next

I proposed a targeted alternative instead of a blanket change: decide which products to bundle for which customers. That is the second write-up below.

Which bundle for which segment? A stratified A/B test: +5% spend over control

telecoms · segmentation engine · stratified A/B test · +5% spend in the targeted segment expand +collapse −

Decision

After the blanket change was shelved, the client still needed to grow revenue from the same base. Which product bundle should be offered to which subscribers?

Answer

Build a segmentation engine that assigns bundles to segments, then test each bundle in a randomised experiment before rolling it out. The first targeted bundle lifted spend in its segment by 5% over control.

Baseline

A randomised control group inside the same segment, over the same period. We assigned treatment and control ourselves. The lift is measured against subscribers who were eligible but not offered the bundle, not against the previous period.

Method

  • Segmentation inputs: usage split by voice and data, in-bundle versus out-of-bundle usage, bundle types bought, monthly spend and spend mix, call duration and data volume.
  • Models: k-means for the segments, LightGBM to choose the bundle for each segment.
  • Experiment: stratified A/B test. Eligible subscribers were split into spend bands, then randomly assigned to treatment or control within each band, so the two groups were balanced on average spend per user before the offer.
  • Outcome: usage and spend in the treatment group compared with the control group over the test window.

Result

+5% spend in the targeted segment relative to control. Read with the first write-up, the two projects are one story: the untargeted change lost money on paper, the targeted one made money in a controlled test.

Sensitivity

The result is for the first bundle in one segment. It does not establish that every bundle in every segment will lift spend. Each bundle is tested before rollout, which is the point of the design.

Limitations

Anonymised. Test duration, group sizes and the confidence interval on the 5% are not reproduced here.

Which customers should we call about a loan? About 11 in 100: R411k profit per 1,000 customers

public bank data · offer targeting · calibrated LightGBM · 95% of the profit ceiling expand +collapse −

Decision

Which customers should a bank's personal-lending team call about a loan offer, and what is the call list worth? The memo is addressed to a fictional Head of Personal Lending. The data is the public Thera Bank dataset of 5,000 customers.

Answer

Call the customers whose calibrated acceptance probability is 10% or higher, which is about 112 in every 1,000. Modelled profit is R411,020 per 1,000 customers, 95% of the R432,000 that perfect foresight would earn.

Baseline

Calling everyone loses R20,000 per 1,000 customers: only 9.6% accept, so the expected margin per call is below the cost of the call. A simple income cutoff earns R315,340. Calling nobody earns R0.

Method

  • Unit economics are stated as assumptions: R500 per call and R5,000 margin per accepted loan. Break-even is a 10% probability (500 / 5,000), so the economics set the threshold rather than a fit.
  • Logistic regression and LightGBM, both Platt-calibrated inside each fold, tuned once on log loss and then frozen.
  • Repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 10 repeats. The model was chosen on mean profit at the 10% threshold.

Result

LightGBM earned the most in all 50 folds: R411,020 per 1,000 customers (SD R6,713), against R342,970 for logistic regression. At the threshold, precision is 83% and recall is 97%. PR-AUC is 0.99, against a 0.096 baseline.

Sensitivity

  • At R1,000 per call, the threshold moves to 20% and profit falls to R358,000. LightGBM still wins.
  • If 25% or 50% of the customers who accept would have borrowed without a call, profit falls to R294,600 or R179,000. This assumes the share is the same for everyone, which flatters targeting.
  • Without the CD Account feature, profit drops by R3,000.

Limitations

Every customer in the data was offered the loan, so there is no control group and the data can't show who would have borrowed anyway. The costs are assumptions, and the data looks partly generated. The next project uses a randomised experiment to close the control-group gap.

Generating new data points with singular value decomposition

M.Sc. minor dissertation · UCT Department of Statistical Sciences · SVD-based synthetic data · three datasets expand +collapse −

Problem

Small datasets limit model development. The question was whether a dataset can be extended with new rows that keep its distribution and its correlations.

Method

Decompose the data matrix with SVD. Keep the components that explain most of the variance as the signal, and treat the component with the smallest singular value as noise. Resample that noise from its empirical distribution and add it back to the signal to produce new rows.

Data

Abalone (4,177 rows, regression), WHO Life Expectancy (2,938 rows, regression) and NBA rookie (1,340 rows, classification).

Result

Generated data from the full training sets matched the originals on distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, reconstruction error) and on downstream model performance, all scored on a held-out test split: R² 0.548 versus 0.567 on Abalone, 0.850 versus 0.858 on Life Expectancy, and accuracy 0.701 versus 0.694 on NBA. Below 50 source rows the similarity broke down.

Limitations

  • Noise is confined to one component, so generated rows cluster near the originals.
  • Binary variables reconstruct worse than continuous ones.
  • The small-sample tests compared a model trained on generated rows against a model trained on all the original data. Two things differ at once: the data is generated, and there is less of it. The fair comparison is against a model trained on the same number of original rows, and that baseline is not in the dissertation. Neither is a comparison with SMOTE or CTGAN.
